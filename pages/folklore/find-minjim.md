### Find Minjim

**Summary.** Whether Minjam Walton of Cumberland County, Virginia (also spelled Minjum, Mingum, and Minjium in the records) is related to Luke F. Walton, and who John T. Walton's father was (the family record calls him John Tabb; no record from his life spells out the T). The proved paternal line ends at John T. Walton (1811–1892), the family’s John Tabb. The situation:

The paternal line is on record from me back to John T. Walton, the family’s John Tabb, born in Virginia in 1811, died in Hale County, Alabama, in 1892. Every link below him has a document. Above him, nothing. His will and his probate name his wife and his children. No record I have found names his father.

There is one candidate. A Cumberland County, Virginia man the clerks spelled Minjam, Minjium, Minchun, Minjum, Mingum and Mygum, who signed himself Minjum, and who as far as any record shows never set foot in Alabama. The most Alabama name ever written down. My uncle’s family record says he was John Tabb’s father. The Daughters of the American Revolution say so too, and give him a middle name. Nobody has ever produced a document that says it.

So, three questions. Who was he? Was he John T. Walton’s father? And does he connect the rest of the line, the one my uncle’s record runs back to 1049? Everything we have is below with the links, each item marked by how close I got to the record: an image I read, an index entry, a typed transcript, or someone’s compilation. If you are holding the paper that answers any of the three, you will know exactly where it fits.

Wanted: This page is a case file, and a case file wants witnesses. Here is what would move it, in order of how badly I want it. Bring one and your name goes on this page beside it, if you want it there. If what you bring kills the story, it goes up anyway.

1. One sentence, in a clerk’s hand, that says John T. Walton was the son of Minjum Walton. Or one that says he was not. The administration, the inventory and the sale have been read, and none of them says it. What is left of the estate is the confirmed account, laid over in March 1840 and not found in the minutes through January 1841, and the original 1840 marriage bonds on Library of Virginia Reel 43.

2. A pair of eyes that can read line 26 of the 1830 Cumberland census. I read Minjum; two agents read Morgan. The crop is on this page. Paleographers, old-hand readers, anyone who grew up reading their grandmother’s letters.

3. The same eyes for the six initials on the six-hands plate: J or T, each beside the same writer’s own J and T. A machine has already been wrong about these letters twice on this page, and I do not trust a machine, mine included, to tell the two apart.

4. Anyone descended from Jesse W. Walton of Goochland, William Mann Walton of Henrico or Mingum H. Walton of Powhatan, who has a Bible, a letter, a photograph with a name on the back, or a story about a brother who went to Alabama.

5. The Walton family Bible that the DAR applicants cited in 1919, which went to Lawton, Oklahoma, with Wyllie Walton Dunlap and has not been seen since.

6. Anyone who can look at one image at a FamilySearch center: the 1858 Goochland marriage register, where Jesse W. Walton’s father is written in a clerk’s hand and an indexer read Morgan.

7. A photograph of J. T. Walton’s stone in Greensboro Cemetery, Hale County, Alabama. Nobody has one.

Who he was:

Born between 1775 and 1784, by the censuses. On 5 February 1806 he gave bond in Cumberland to marry Sally F. Mann, with her father’s consent and Benjamin Fuqua and William Mann Jr. as sureties; the clerk wrote his name so badly that Eggleston, abstracting the bond in 1931, printed “Minjinn (?)” and FamilySearch indexed him as Miryium. In 1810 he is Minchun Walton with two small boys, a page from John H. Parker, Jesse Parker and William F. Mann. In 1817 he witnessed John Holt Parker’s deed of trust beside Jesse Parker. In 1820 he is Minjum, with two boys under ten, two of ten to sixteen, and a girl.

A compiled sketch says it: In 1827 he bought 72 acres from Jesse Parker. The deed has not been found. In 1830 the line I read as Minjum, and two agents read as Morgan, has a man and a woman of forty to fifty, one boy of fifteen to twenty, four boys under ten, a girl, and one enslaved woman. In 1831 he sold 85 acres to a Jesse Walton, bounded by John H. Parker, Bartlett Cox and Robert Robinson. In 1836 he gave 23 cents to the Jenkins church. On 10 March 1838 he witnessed an apprenticeship indenture and signed it himself, large and flourished: Minjum Walton. The paper was the county binding out a free woman’s children to a neighbor, Minjum as witness; the overseer of the poor who signed it was Edward George Walton, born about 1778, who died in 1840 leaving a will, a widow and nine children, and whose own father is not on paper. If he was a late son of George and Margaret Tabb, he and Minjum were brothers. In 1839 he is Mingum on the tax list, for the last time.

Then the household is gone. In the 1840 census no Walton of any of his spellings heads a household in Cumberland. A Sarah Walton, fifty to sixty, with a boy of ten to fifteen and one enslaved woman, sits among Cox, Robinson and the Parkers, the men who bounded the 85 acres in 1831. On 8 March 1840 a Sarah F. Walton consented, alone, to her daughter Elizabeth M.’s marriage to Jesse D. Parker. The family record says he died in 1839. Letters on the estate of Minjam Walton were granted on 23 December 1839, so a man of that name was dead by then. The letters do not name a widow or a child. One thing still cuts against calling that man the 1806 groom: a Benj H Walton kept the new Oak Forest post office in Cumberland through 1842, and a B. H. Walton of Cumberland was in Richmond in 1846. Whether that was him, a younger man of the same initials, or another man is open.

No record names his children. One register names a father, and the father it names is not written Minjum: William M. Walton, born about 1809, whose 1860 Goochland marriage entry calls his parents Benjamin H. Walton and Sally. That is a son of a Benjamin H. and a Sally. He is Minjum’s son only if Minjum was Benjamin, which is the theory below and not a record. The trees add a list. Jesse W., born 1807, died in Goochland in 1889, and an index of his 1858 marriage reads his father as Morgan. The 1831 buyer is a Jesse Walton, and the deed does not say that Jesse is this one. Elizabeth M. Walton married Jesse D. Parker in 1840 with the consent of her mother, Sarah F. Walton. Then a Mingum H. born about 1820, the junior of the church books, who farmed in Powhatan until 1880. John T., born about 1811, and Joseph M., born about 1814, are on that list only if the stories below are right. The family record calls the first of them John Tabb. William M. named a son Minjum Howard in 1868, and that grandson carried the name through five censuses to his death in 1944. A name passed down does not name Minjum’s children.

The name, my theory:

My theory: Minjam is Benjamin. A diminutive, the way a Southside Virginia family said it at home. Benj’m. Swallow the first syllable and the last and the word that comes out is the one on the bond, and the clerks wrote what they heard, each a little differently. The one record that names a Benjamin H. Walton as a father is William M. Walton’s 1860 marriage register, which calls William that man’s son, and that William named his own boy Minjum Howard. Whether the Benjamin H. is Minjum is the theory. It would also explain why a search for Benjamin Walton, Cumberland, 1806, returns nothing: the bond is indexed under Miryium. Some of the dead ends were the wrong syllables.

Against it, two things, one of them heavy. He signed his own name on an 1838 indenture, and he signed it Minjum. And in ninety years of records for the two later Mingums, the Powhatan man and the Richmond grandson, four censuses each, a muster roll, a marriage index, a death index and the city directories, no clerk ever wrote Benjamin for either of them; the church books have a Mincham and a Mingum junior and no Benjamin either. The other readings stay on the table: a real given name nobody can place, handed down because it was theirs. One register would settle it, Jesse W. Walton’s of 1858, which names his father and which an indexer read as Morgan. Nobody has seen the page. Virginia kept no death register before 1853. The court did keep an estate. On 23 December 1839 it granted letters on Minjam Walton. The inventory and the January 1840 sale name a widow and a buyer named John Walton, and neither calls him a son. A settlement report was laid over on 23 March 1840 and was not confirmed in the minutes read through 25 January 1841. The confirmed account, the one that would name heirs, has not been found.

Was he John T. Walton’s father? The case for Minjum, then the other stories:

The DAR story: William Walton Jr. of Amherst. The claim: The story, and it is unsupported. The applications say William Walton Jr., Revolutionary War pensioner of Strawberry Hill, Greene County, Alabama, had a son Minjam H. Walton who married Sarah Mann, and that Minjam’s son was John Tabb Walton.

For it: Six DAR applications say so, filed from about 1919 into the 2020s and re-certified across a century. The patriot is real. His pension file is real. His grave at Strawberry Hill is marked. John T. Walton did marry in Greene County in 1847, where William Jr.’s daughters were marrying Glovers, Creswells and Webbs.

Against it: The authority those applications cite is “Family Bible and traditions.” When I went to buy the supporting documents in June 2026, there were none on file. The record copy of an application costs $15. The supporting-documents option, which costs $30 and appears only when a Bible page, deed or will was submitted, does not appear on the Minjam-line applications. Nobody ever filed the paper. William Jr.’s documented children are Amelia Tillman, Justina, Louisa, John Gennerick, and Jane. That list comes from the UNC Walton Family Papers, the 1946 Tillman family history, and the Strawberry Hill burials; Find a Grave cites the 1919 DAR Magazine for it, and the Magazine has no such list. No Minjam. No Benjamin. His own sworn deposition, Greene County, 12 January 1833, names his father William, his brother Tilman and his uncle Jesse, and no son. It puts him in Augusta County, Virginia, from 1784 to 1791, Burke County, North Carolina, from 1791 to 1800, and Charleston, South Carolina, from 1800 to 1815. John T. Walton was born in Virginia around 1811. A son can be raised by Virginia kin while his father is in Charleston. It is friction, not a wall. And there is a homonym. William Jr. did have a son John: John Gennerick Walton, born about 1811, married Elizabeth Ann Glover in 1841, died in Texas about 1870. Different middle name, different death, different birthplace. He is not the man who died in Hale County in 1892. He is exactly the coincidence that lets a compiler weld two Johns born in 1811 into one, and then reach for a Minjam to explain the Tabb the family wrote in later. Then the arithmetic, which came last and should have come first. William Jr. was born in January 1767. The 1810 and 1820 censuses put Minjum’s birth between 1775 and 1784, and the 1830 line, if it is his, between 1780 and 1784. The family record says 1779. A son born when the father was eight to seventeen. The 1946 Tillman family history gives William Jr. nine children by two wives, the eldest born in 1795, and no Minjam, no Benjamin, no Minjum.

Verdict: Unsupported, and on the dates next to impossible. A real patriot spliced onto a Cumberland man by people who believed it and wrote down that they were going on tradition. Not formally disproved, because no document says Minjam was anyone else’s son. A father at twelve is where I stop pretending this is open.

The case for Minjum. The claim: The case, and it is unproved. George Walton of Cumberland County married Margaret Tabb in 1759. The story says one of their sons was Minjum, that Minjum married Sally F. Mann in 1806, and that their son, born about 1811, was John T. Walton, called John Tabb for his grandmother’s family. No record from his life spells Tabb, and no record names his father.

For it: The name the story uses. Tabb is a Cumberland surname. Thomas Tabb (1719–1782) left a will naming his daughter Walton; George and Margaret had a son they called Langhorn Tabb Walton, who posted a marriage bond in 1786; when George died intestate in 1817, Langhorn T. administered the estate with a John Tabb standing surety. Those Tabbs were real. Nothing from John T. Walton’s life says he was given their name. Kerns lists a Minjum among George’s seven children, and the Minjum of the records was really there: the 1806 bond, the 1820 household with two boys under ten, the land in 1827 and 1831. A John T. Walton married Jane D. Hudgins in Cumberland on 23 March 1840, with Jesse D. Parker as surety, and that June a “Jno T Walton,” twenty to thirty, headed a three-person Cumberland household. In 1850, John T. Walton’s household in Greene County had a daughter Sarah J., 9, born in Virginia about 1841, and Lucy, 7, born in Virginia about 1843; the next child, Betty, was born in Alabama about 1846; and he married Sarah F. Gills in Alabama in 1847. A first wife who died around 1845, a widower who moved south with two small daughters and remarried: the shape fits. The Hudgens kin went to Missouri in those same years. This John T. did not. In August 1841 the Cumberland groom was still in Cumberland: he and “Jane, formerly Hudgins” sold her 75 acres, bounded by William E. Carrington, with her brothers and sisters, and he acknowledged the deed before the county justices on the 26th. The Alabama man’s first daughter was born in Virginia that year, and Jane D.’s sisters were a Sally and a Lucy. Then what October 2026 added. The surety on that 1840 bond, Jesse D. Parker, had given bond to marry Elizabeth M. Walton, daughter of Sarah F. Walton, fifteen days earlier; Sarah F. is, on my reading, Minjum’s Sally F. Mann, consenting alone the year after his last tax entry. The 1830 census gives the household on the line I take to be Mingum Walton’s, if it is his, exactly one boy born between 1810 and 1815. And the J. M. Walton on the page before John T.’s in Pole Cat Beat is Joseph M. Walton, born about 1814, who married Lucy Gills in Amelia in 1838, Sarah Frances’s sister by the compiled list, and whose 1888 Greensboro estate the June file had mistaken for another family’s. Two Walton men marrying two Gills sisters and settling beside their father-in-law: the trees call them brothers and Minjum’s sons. And the Gills family reaches back into Cumberland: Jesse D. Parker, the surety on the 1840 bond and Sarah F. Walton’s son-in-law, married a third Gills sister, Mary Jane, in Amelia in 1845.

Against it: No document names John T. Walton, or the family record’s John Tabb Walton, as the son of Minjum, Mingum, Minjium or Benjamin H. Walton. Not the 1806 bond, not the 1831 deed, not the 1817 estate, not the 1839 administration, not the 1840 inventory and sale, not his own will. I have looked where such a sentence would be and it is not there. The one proved child of Benjamin H. and Sally is William M. Walton, born about 1809 in Cumberland, who married in Goochland in 1860 and died in Henrico in 1883. He never left Virginia. Neither, as far as the record shows, did anyone else in the cluster: Mingum is on the 1839 tax list, a Benjamin H. Walton is in Cumberland court dockets for decades, and the later Mingums are in Powhatan and Richmond. Nothing puts any of them in Alabama. The 1840 groom is identified by initials and chronology, not by an age. The original bond, which would carry a groom’s age or a parent’s consent, is on a Library of Virginia microfilm I have not read. And the initial is a problem. Three printed abstracts of the bond, Eggleston in 1931, Hart in 1933 and Elliott in 1969, read John J.; Raider’s transcription of the 1841 deed reads the signature John J. Walton; the 1840 census and FamilySearch’s abstract register read T. Two Cumberland documents, read by four people, give J. Every Alabama record gives T, and the one Alabama hand that wavers, the 1850 enumerator’s, looks like a J. Either the Cumberland man was John J. Walton, a different man who stayed, or one man’s initial was read two ways on two sides of a state line. The original bond and the deed book would settle it. And the Cumberland trees are a mess. FamilySearch gives George Walton sixteen children, including a “Minjum H T Walton” listed as female with zero sources. Kerns gives seven, with Maurice and Minjum as two people; the Tabb compilation gives four, with an “M. H. T.” born 1765 and no Minjum at all. The 1817 estate names one son as administrator and no distributees in the pages read so far. Whether Minjum was George and Margaret Tabb’s son at all rests on Kerns, unread, and on the name. And every strong fact here is about other people. They put Jesse D. Parker inside Minjum’s household and Joseph M. Walton beside John T. None of them puts a pen to paper over the words “John T. Walton, son of.”

Verdict: Unproved, and the story the documents lean on. A Minjum who was really there, a Sarah F. Walton consenting in 1840, read as his widow, her daughter’s husband on the groom’s bond two weeks later, a household with a boy of the right age in every census that is his, a man the trees call a brother who married the bride’s sister, and that same Parker marrying a third sister in 1845. Against it, the initial does not sit still: three abstracts and a typed deed read the groom as John J., the 1840 census reads T, and on 27 July 1840 the Cumberland court wrote John T. Walton in a debt suit that does not say he is the groom. The Alabama man is John T. in every record of his. What is missing is one sentence of filiation. The administration, the inventory and the sale have been read, and none of them names an heir. The settlement was laid over and not confirmed in the minutes through January 1841. The original 1840 bonds have not been opened.

Neither. The claim: Open. John T. Walton, whom the family record calls John Tabb, may have come from some other Virginia Walton line, both of the stories above being later families reaching for him.

For it: Under the name John Tabb he is absent from every Cumberland record. The 1840 marriage is a John T., not a John Tabb, and the T is an abstractor’s reading. Virginia had Walton clusters in Amherst, Hanover, Goochland, Powhatan and Cumberland, and a man born there in 1811 could have come from any of them.

Against it: Two families that did not know each other, the DAR applicants in Alabama and the tree-builders working from Cumberland, both reached for a Minjam or Minjum married to a Mann. That is easier to explain if one real tradition sits under both than if each invented him. And the Tabb the later record writes into his name did not come from nowhere.

Verdict: Open. Less likely than the Cumberland case, and not ruled out by anything.

Born outside marriage. The claim: Open. John T. Walton’s father was not a Walton. His mother was one, and he was born outside marriage, carrying her name; or she married a Walton who raised him under his.

For it: Nothing in the record contradicts it, which is not the same as support. A son born outside marriage, a stepson raised under his stepfather’s name, a boy raised by an uncle or a grandfather: any of these breaks a father-to-son chain without leaving a mark in the records that name fathers, because those records assume the ordinary case. One thing to look at squarely. In this period a child born outside marriage was sometimes given the father’s surname as a middle name and the mother’s as a surname. If his middle name was Tabb, he would fit that pattern: a Walton mother and a Tabb father. He fits the ordinary pattern just as well if a grandmother was Margaret Tabb. The name, which no record from his life spells out, does not choose between them.

Against it: There is no record for it either. A father’s name missing from the trail of a man born in Virginia in 1811 is the ordinary condition of the records, not a sign of anything. A Tabb in his name, if he had one, is the kind of thing the Cumberland Walton who married a Tabb in 1759 could explain. It would not prove the name, and it would not prove the father.

Verdict: Open. No document for it and none against it. Where it would show: the Cumberland order books of about 1810 to 1815, where bastardy bonds and apprenticeships were entered, and the one test I am not going to take.

A name change. The claim: He, or his father, went by another name earlier, and the records that would name his father are filed under it.

For it: The spelling cluster is itself a name change, one generation up: if Minjam is Benjamin, then a man appears in the records under a name his own family did not use. The same thing could have happened to the son. A John Walton who was John Tabb to the people who knew him, or a boy born a Tabb who took a stepfather’s Walton, or a middle name that the family filled in later. He is John T. in every record from his lifetime; the T is not spelled out on any of them. And the search has been run under one name. Every pass through the Cumberland books looked for a Walton. A John Tabb, born about 1811, with no Walton on him, would not have been seen.

Against it: By March 1840 he is John T. Walton on a Cumberland marriage bond, if that groom is him, and nothing has been found under any other name before it. Virginia had no name-change procedure to speak of; people changed their names by using a new one, which leaves no paper unless a deed or a court order says “formerly” or “alias.” None does, so far.

Verdict: Open. The same order books and deeds as the story above, read for a John Tabb without the Walton or a Walton with an alias. Its one instruction to the search: stop assuming the surname.

The graft: the right family under the wrong name. The claim: The John Walton who died in Hale County in 1892 is Luke’s ancestor, and the pedigree above him, John Tabb, son of Minjam, son of William Walton Jr., was attached to him by a later hand and belongs to someone else, or to no one.

For it: Luke’s, on 14 October 2026: “I think we have spent a fuck ton of time looking for John Tabb when it should be John J.” No record made while the man lived spells Tabb. The earliest dated Tabb is his granddaughter Willie Walton Dunlap’s DAR filing of about 1921, twenty-nine years after his death, and every later filing copies her line. The hands that wrote his initial with him in the room do not agree, two of them cannot be read at all, and his son was John Julian: J. Julian in 1870, Julian J. at his wedding and in 1910, John J. in the will. A J handed down, if the father’s letter was a J. Luke’s own line repeats a name the same way, Francis to Mark Francis to Luke Francis. The Cumberland groom of 1840 is John J. Walton in three printed abstracts and on the 1841 deed as Raider typed it. If the Alabama man was a John J., the Cumberland match stands without Tabb, and it stands without Minjum, because the bond names the bride’s mother and not the groom’s father. The whole weight of the Minjum link is the family record and the DAR line, which the DAR itself flags as unverified.

Against it: Two hands with him in the room wrote T beyond argument, the county clerk who licensed his marriage in 1847, with his own J one entry up, and the registration clerk of 1867, with his J beside it; the enumerator of 1880 wrote the letter he wrote for Lucy T.; and every printed, typed and indexed record of his life says T, as does the stone. The family said T at the door in 1880 and to the clerk in 1867, and named a grandson Jack Thomas. A graft explains Tabb; it does not explain the T. The family record could be a graft and the Cumberland groom could still be him, or neither; the two questions come apart. What this story changes is the search, not the answer. It takes Tabb off the list of things known and puts the 1892 man’s own name at the head of it.

Verdict: Open, and the search runs under it from here: record each initial as the hand wrote it, J or T, and never normalise; search the Virginia books for John Walton, John J. and John T. alike, and for Jno.; find the earliest writing that calls him Tabb and whose hand it was; and read his own hand, the oath book of 1867 and the deed book of 1841, which can say J or T with no clerk in between.

What is proved below him:

The proved paternal line ends at John T. Walton, whom the family record calls John Tabb; no record from his life spells the T out, and the hands that wrote it with him in the room do not agree on the letter. He married Sarah F. Gills in Greene County, Alabama, on 15 April 1847, by license of the 13th; farmed Pole Cat Beat, Perry County, in 1860, Virginia-born, 49, with six children; told the 1880 census taker both his parents were born in Virginia; and died in Greensboro on 21 July 1892. His will and probate name his wife, sons John J. and Joseph P., daughters Lucy C. Ricard and Alice Walker, and a grandson, Urbin Lovell. The rest is on the family page with its sources.

John Julian Walton is the son the line runs through, then Jack Thomas Walton, then my grandfather Frank, then my father Mark, then me. Those links are federal paper: Social Security records, census pages, and the Alabama Supreme Court opinions on Jack’s murder. They are not the mystery. The mystery is one generation up.

One more thing belongs at the top, not the bottom. The 1850 slave schedule of Greene County lists John T. Walton as holding three people, a woman of twenty-five and two children of four and two; the 1860 slave schedule lists John T. Walton of Pole Cat beat as holding fifteen, a man of fifty-eight, a woman of forty-five, and thirteen women, girls and boys from thirty-two years down to an infant of three months. The schedules name him John T. Walton, the man this page is about. I say so on the family page and I will say it here: chattel slavery was an atrocity, and no amount of interest in who his father was changes what he did with his life.

Where he came from, scored:

Virginia: Documented. The 1850, 1860, 1870 and 1880 censuses have him born in Virginia. The 1880 census has both of his parents born there. His children said the same in eleven of thirteen census statements between 1880 and 1920, and none of them ever named a county.

The move, about 1843 to 1846: Documented. Sarah J. (about 1841) and Lucy (about 1843) were born in Virginia. Betty (about 1846) was born in Alabama. He married in Greene County in 1847.

Cumberland County: Unconfirmed. The 1840 Cumberland marriage of a John T. Walton fits him in every particular the record gives, the 1840 census has that John T. heading a newlywed household there, and in August 1841 the same groom signed a Cumberland deed with his wife. No record says it is him. Three printed abstracts of the 1840 bond, and a typed transcription of the 1841 deed, read the groom’s initial as J. On 27 July 1840 the Cumberland court wrote John T. Walton as defendant in a debt suit it dismissed. That order does not say he is the groom, the buyer at the estate sale, or anyone’s son. In Alabama the printed, indexed and clerk-copied records make it T, and the five hands that wrote it with him in the room give two T beyond argument, the county clerk of 1847 and the registration clerk of 1867, a T by match in 1880, a hand in 1850 that wrote the letter as its J on the population page and as its T on the slave schedule the same day, and two hands, 1860 and 1870, whose J and T are one shape. A second 1840 John T. Walton, in Halifax County, keeps Cumberland from being the only fit.

Tabb: Unproved. No record made while he lived spells his middle name. The earliest writing that calls him John Tabb is his granddaughter’s DAR application of about 1921, and the five later applications copy her line. Of the hands that wrote his initial with him in the room, two made a T beyond argument, one a T by match, one a J by match, and two cannot be read.

Minjam: Open. No document names the father of the John Walton who died in Hale County in 1892.

So the joke was historically accurate. They really did leave Virginia for Alabama, and the paper trail turned into a nightmare at the state line. They should have stayed in Virginia.

What is off in the record:

The bottom six rows are right. John T. to me, every link with a document on the family page. My grandfather’s name and birth year are off by a little; the record says Francis James, 1919, and his Social Security record says Frank James, 29 November 1918.

The row above them is the whole case, and it is probably two men. “Minjam H. T. Walton (1779-1839).” The Tabb family compilation gives George Walton and Margaret Tabb a son born in 1765 called “M. H. T.” or Maurice H. Tabb Walton; the online trees merged him with Minjam. My uncle’s row keeps Minjam’s dates and Maurice’s initials, and the censuses put his birth a year or so off 1779, and 1839 is his last tax entry. Letters on Minjam Walton were granted that December, so the death year is a court date and not only the tax list. The initials are still Maurice’s. None of that says who told my uncle the dates, or that this man was George’s son, or that he was John T. Walton’s father.

A father at twelve. The record has William Walton Jr. born in 1767 and Minjam in 1779. The censuses put Minjam between 1775 and 1784. William Jr. would have been eight to seventeen. Nobody in the family, and nobody at the DAR, seems to have subtracted.

The splice. The record puts Minjam under William Walton Jr. of Amherst and Greene County; the courthouse records put Minjam in Cumberland. Nothing on paper joins them. The 1946 Tillman family history lists William Jr.’s nine children by two wives, and none is a Minjam or a Benjamin.

The titles. “Youngest Soldier Serving in the Revolutionary Army”: his own deposition has him in service at thirteen and fourteen, young and not provably youngest. “Brother of the Grandfather of Declaration Signer George Walton”: a collateral claim nobody has checked against a document. “Sir John Walton of Devon”: a minister’s son styled Sir, and whether he is the Virginia John at all is a long-running dispute in the trees.

The top of it. Two men who died in Hungary two centuries apart, a Richard who died in Bosnia in 1463, a William who died in 1581 with a son born in 1583. My uncle put a question mark on that row himself, and on one other. I have no documents for any of it. What the Victoria County History says about the Lancashire manor is that the last Walton to hold it left daughters.

Readings, not findings:

Three censuses, one household, and room for a boy born in 1811. Put the 1810, 1820 and 1830 lines side by side. In 1810, Minchun Walton has two boys under ten: the trees’ Jesse W., born 1807, and William Mann, born 1809, fit those ages. In 1820, Minjum Walton has two boys of ten to sixteen and two under ten, and a girl under ten: those two, thirteen and eleven, fit the first pair; a boy born in 1811 and one born in 1814 would fill the second; the girl would fit the Elizabeth M. who married Jesse D. Parker in 1840. An earlier read had the ten-to-sixteen column empty; the frame has two there, and the compiled ages fit those columns. In 1830, the line I read as Minjum, and two agents as Morgan, has exactly one boy of fifteen to twenty, four boys under ten, one girl of ten to fifteen, and a man and woman of forty to fifty. If it is his: one boy born between 1810 and 1815 still at home, who could be John T. or Joseph M. and not both; the other was nineteen or sixteen and somewhere else. And four boys born in the 1820s, where the compiled list has one, the Mingum H. who was thirty in 1850; the list is short by three sons, and the 1840 household’s boy of ten to fifteen is one of them if that household is his. The two eldest were gone, as men of twenty-one and twenty-three would be. The head’s birth falls between 1775 and 1784 on the first two counts, and between 1780 and 1784 if the third is his. None of this names anyone. It says the household the records give Minjum had room for sons in every slot the compiled list uses, every time anyone counted, with the 1830 count held at arm’s length until someone reads that name better than I can.

Edward George, the man beside him: cousin, or brother. Two Cumberland Waltons born within a few years of each other, 1778 and about 1780, on the same census pages from 1810 to 1830, signing the same indenture in March 1838, one as overseer of the poor and one as witness. Edward George Walton had the elevated life: two marriages, nine children, twenty-seven enslaved people, a seat among the justices, a will proved the month after the two 1840 bonds. Minjum had the deed books and the tax list. The compiled trees made Edward George a son of the older Edward Walton who died in 1807, and the better-kept profile now says that attachment was a merger and is probably wrong, because the 1807 will names seven children and no Edward. That leaves a man named Edward George with no father, in a family where George Walton and Margaret Tabb had sons born in the 1760s and, if Kerns and the censuses are right about Minjum, at least one more fifteen years later. The arithmetic that allows Minjum allows Edward George. If they were both George’s late sons, the 1838 sheet is two brothers, and George’s 1817 estate settlement would show both taking shares. Luke’s own thought, and it is his: a middle name like George, passed down in a family that reused it, is exactly the kind of thing that turns into “George Walton was my grandfather” two generations later, whether or not he was. I would not build on that. I would read Will Book 6.

The 1831 deed is a sale, not a child list. Minjum sold 85 acres to Jesse Walton for $350. The file has treated this as Minjum naming an heir, and as a slot that John T. does not fill. A sale for money names a buyer. That Jesse could be the Jesse W. the sketch calls Minjum’s eldest son. He could also be George Walton’s son Jesse, Minjum’s brother in Kerns’s list, who was running a store in Powhatan around 1800. The deed does not say. So it does not count against John T., and it does not count for anyone. It says a Minjum Walton owned land in Cumberland in 1831 and that a Jesse Walton bought it.

Jesse W. and William M., whom the trees call the brothers who stayed, and the register that names a Morgan. The two eldest sons the trees give Minjum never left Virginia. In 1860 William M. Walton, 50, was living in Jesse W. Walton’s house in Goochland, in the year William married Margaret Seay and a clerk wrote his parents down as Benjamin H. and Sally. The 1831 deed has a Jesse Walton buying Minjum’s 85 acres, and the trees make that Jesse this one. So far, so much like brothers. Then the one register that names Jesse W.’s own parents, his 1858 marriage, was indexed as Morgan Walton and Hannah Walton, with a second indexer reading the mother as Sarah. If the clerk wrote Minjum and the indexer saw Morgan, which is exactly what happened to a verifier on the 1830 census line, then the family stands as the trees have it: Jesse W. in 1807, William M. in 1809, and the two who went to Alabama after. If the clerk wrote Morgan, the eldest son belongs to another man, the 1831 buyer may be that man’s son, and the 1830 line goes with him. That Jesse is, by every mark the records give, Jesse D.: in 1850 his wife is Mary, 27, Elizabeth M. is gone, and the bill filed after his death in 1877 names his widow Mary J. and a first child Sarah F. So the man who stood surety for John T. Walton in 1840, as Sarah F. Walton’s new son-in-law, was, five years later, if the Amelia groom is the same man, the brother-in-law of Lucy Tate Gills, who had married Joseph M. Walton, and of Sarah Frances Gills, who would marry John T. Walton in Alabama. The Gills family that both Alabama Waltons married into was already tied, on this reading, to Minjum’s neighborhood in Virginia, through the one man who stood on John T.’s bond. The chancery cases make Jesse D. a man of John T.’s own generation, a son of an older Jesse Parker, probably the Jesse who sold Minjum his land in 1827, and name no Walton. None of this is a sentence that says “John T. Walton, son of Minjum.” If Sarah F. is the widow and the groom is her son, the 1840 groom is standing inside Minjum’s household. The paper does not say either of those things.

Where “Tabb” comes from. The Cumberland story leans on his middle name, so here is where the middle name comes from. Every record from his lifetime that I have read gives it as an initial: John T. The “Mingum Walton, 4th Regiment” of a family-history roster did not come from this book, and its source is unstated.

A family Bible, the one the DAR applicants cited: The DAR’s Bible database has 118 Walton registers and none for this family; its every-name index of chapter-transcribed records has no Minjam, Minjum, Mingum or John Tabb Walton under any spelling. The Library of Virginia’s Bible collection has a Cumberland Walton Bible, and it is Henry C. Walton’s, the 1865 collision. The FamilySearch profiles for John Tabb and his children carry no memories at all. If the Bible exists it went down John Julian’s line to Lawton, Oklahoma, with Wyllie Walton Dunlap, and nobody has transcribed it.

The 1850 censuses of Cumberland, Goochland, Powhatan and Buckingham, every line, for a Sarah or Sally Walton in her sixties: None. The widow of 1840, if that is who she was, is not a head of household or a visible boarder in any of the four counties ten years later. Henrico and Richmond were downloaded and not screened; Amelia’s index has no Walton at all.

FamilySearch’s Virginia registers after 1853, rendered without a login, for a second record naming the 1806 groom as somebody’s father: One, and it names a Morgan: Jesse W. Walton’s 1858 marriage, with the image restricted to FamilySearch centers. William M.’s 1883 death register, Sally’s own death entry, and the Alabama death indexes for John Julian and Sarah F. did not turn up by any open route. Lucy C. Ricard’s did, once signed in: father John T. Walton, no mother indexed, the image at a center. The Minjum Howard Walton 1944 death index did, and names his parents William Walton and Margaret Seay.

Faulkner County, Arkansas, for Urbin Lovell’s parents: The county marriage index has no Lovell marriage before 1933. His parents, probably Fannie E. Walton and William M. Lovell, married in Hale County in 1879, so there was nothing to find in Arkansas but the boy.

Chronicling America, every Virginia page for Walton with Cumberland, 1836 to 1850, for an estate, a sale or a death notice under any of his spellings: None. No Minjum, Minjam or Mingum Walton anywhere in the Library of Congress newspapers, 1800 to 1880. The one B. H. Walton of Cumberland in those years is a hotel arrival in Richmond in September 1846, which went into the ledger as friction, not as proof.

The Walton Y-DNA surname project, for a published signature of any of these lines: None on the open web for George and Margaret Tabb’s line, for Benjamin H. and William Mann Walton’s, or for the Amherst and Greene County patriot’s. Moot, since I am not testing, but logged.

Two citations the machine invented:

Two citations in this file were invented by the machine, and I want them on the page. The first was “Piedmont Virginia Historical Collection, Item 276,” said to name a William Mann Walton, a Benjamin H. Walton and a Herman Hidmore Walton in one tidy household. No such collection exists. The second was “Texas & P. Ry. Co. v. Smith, 67 F. 524,” said to describe the railroad wreck behind the guardianship that sits next to John Tabb’s probate petition. The citation resolves to a Louisiana land-title case with no Paoli, no wreck and no guardian.

Both read beautifully. Both were false. The lesson stuck: a snippet reading “John Tabb Walton, son of…” is the most seductive and least verified thing a research agent can hand you. Since then every claim here has had to show the image, the index entry, or the transcript it came from, and the ledger above says which of the three it is, every time. The same rule caught a human error when a second look at the 1860 frame turned a Walton neighbor into a Walker; that one is under readings. I write about machines and answerability for a living. This is what it looks like at the kitchen table.

The name:

Whatever the Cumberland clerks were trying to write, I kept it. Minjim Minjum is an Alabama royal claimant in The Signatories, the novel I wrote with Jonathan Gillie. The character owes the man his name and nothing else. That does not make the man an ancestor, and the name was too good to leave in a deed book.
