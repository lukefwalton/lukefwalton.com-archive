hostNote
Last week I asked whether a newborn can already predict music, on [What Babies Listen To](https://lukefwalton.com/love-music-more/episodes/what-babies-listen-to/). This one stays with that question and aims it at rhythm, then at monkeys. The factory-default version is: how much musical expectation do we show up with? Winkler and colleagues, in 2009, played rhythmic patterns to babies a few days old. Their brains reacted hard to downbeats. I put that next to James Brown and the one: hit the one and people have something to dance to. *Wheels on the Bus* works the same way. The newborns were not only memorizing a string of noises. Inside a short phrase they were building a picture of where the pattern should go, and an unexpected move lit the brain up. I had thought that arrived later. I will not make the same claim for pitch. I have not found a newborn study with a clear equivalent for an out-of-key note. Timing shows up. What counts as pleasant may still be the cultural part. A friend, Jordan, did this kind of work in a neuroscience program and said the monkeys were the worst coworkers. Some days they did not want to come in. That is the right caution for the rhesus macaque result. Two animals showed sensitivity to unexpected timing. That is not a sweeping evolutionary proof. They might be savants. It is still interesting if a basic auditory system is already keeping a statistical model of sound, the way a footstep has a when. Speech, culture, and a life of listening turn that seed into what we call music. I do not want to call it innate and stop. The missing piece is what the baby had already heard: the mother's voice, speech rhythms, the heartbeat. Prenatal sound might be the first training set. From there, early humans are easier for me to picture around a fire, drumming, than as a cave painting, because I have been at that fire. If there is alien music, it is probably ordered frequencies over time too, even if their ears are not ours. We cannot know. It is still a good door to leave open.

faq
  • question
    Do newborns already respond to rhythm?
    answer
    On the study I talk through here, yes for timing. Winkler and colleagues, in 2009, played rhythmic patterns to babies a few days old, and the brains reacted strongly to downbeats. I do not make the same claim for an out-of-key pitch. I have not found a newborn result that clear.
  • question
    Do monkeys have rhythm?
    answer
    The rhesus macaque result I am using is small. Two animals showed sensitivity to unexpected timing. I will not turn that into a theory of how music evolved. A friend who worked with monkeys in a neuroscience program also said they were awful coworkers, which is its own kind of data.
  • question
    Is musical expectation innate?
    answer
    I do not know, and I say so on the episode. A newborn may already be organizing a short pattern, and prenatal sound, the heartbeat, a voice, speech rhythm, may have trained that before birth. Culture does the rest. The door is open. The proof is not.
