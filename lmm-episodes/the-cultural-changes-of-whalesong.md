  This is the end of the run. [What Babies Listen To](https://lukefwalton.com/love-music-more/episodes/what-babies-listen-to/), [Fishbowls of Influence](https://lukefwalton.com/love-music-more/episodes/fishbowls-of-influence/), [Monkey Studies](https://lukefwalton.com/love-music-more/episodes/monkey-studies/). The question is still what we show up with and what a culture trains. This week is birds. In 2009, researchers showed that Snowball the cockatoo did more than bob along. They sped the song up and slowed it down, and Snowball sped up and slowed down with it. Ten years later, Snowball was coming up with dance moves to different music. I like the puncture. Zebra finches learn their songs, so the song is being copied, passed on. A 2021 study had male zebra finches keep track of regular sequences through substantial tempo changes. That is the part I care about. Happy Birthday survives every key, and the keys between the keys, and it survives being sung fast or slow.

  The song is the relationship. That is what [Giosuè Greco](https://lukefwalton.com/love-music-more/episodes/giosue-greco/) and I had just been saying: a great song is a great song on any instrument, and it does not even need to be the greatest instrument. Snowball and the finches are tracking that relationship across a change in timing, the same statistical expectation I talked about with newborns. Where will the rhythm fall. Where will the melody go. Some parrots can lock their bodies to a beat. Then I went back to monkeys. A 2025 paper I had just read is called Monkeys Have Rhythm. Two adult macaques were trained to tap a click on a microphone, then tested on real music, and they synced those taps to a subjective beat. The earlier macaque result was brain activation at an irregularity. This one is participation, a small one, and it took a lot of training. A dog tapping a foot would still stop me. The machinery is there, the way it is there for Snowball. I can do more than tap for treats. It leaves me feeling tangled up with the music, and it leaves the alien question as a thought experiment: would they have music. The signs, across more than mammals, point at maybe.

  Then the whales, which is the title. One population's song gets replaced by another population's song, and researchers watch those songs move across the South Pacific for years. There is a Billboard Hot 100 of whale song. A 2022 study followed six song types from Eastern Australia to New Caledonia. The New Caledonian whales sang them with high fidelity. They were singing along to the original, a cultural migration, and the songs have rhythmic organization inside them. They are a sequence, with time in them.

  The part I want to leave on is the pattern matching. Researchers took eight years of humpback recordings and ran something like the way a human infant finds words: syllables that keep arriving together get treated as a chunk. They found statistically coherent chunks in the songs. I am calling that word-like. They kind of have lyrics, and I will leave the language claim there. I have a song about a whale and I cannot remember the record. It is [I Am a Lobster, I Am a Barnacle](https://lukefwalton.com/songs/i-am-a-lobster-i-am-a-barnacle/), on [Little Hug](https://lukefwalton.com/albums/little-hug/). The whales remember theirs.
