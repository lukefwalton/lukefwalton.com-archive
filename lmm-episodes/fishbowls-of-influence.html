hostNote
Do good chords exist? Bad ones? If they did, how would anyone know? I take the case of the Tsimane', an Indigenous people in the Bolivian Amazon, and what their listening does to a Western idea of which chords are naturally pleasant. The comparison is the one music theory treats as settled: consonant against dissonant, the intervals a Western ear is taught to hear as pretty or tense. Headphones go in. The result I care about is a different psychoacoustic response, not a confirmation of the textbook ranking. Preference can be a growing habit inside a tradition. A bunch of neat ratios does not settle it. The Tsimane' are not a time capsule, and not a control group to talk over. Their songs carry stories. Communities in this work are reachable by canoe. The lesson is that "pleasant" is something a culture trains, not something a chord possesses. I call the surrounding condition a fishbowl. You do not notice the water. Western ears swim in a harmony pedagogy, a recording tradition, a set of intervals heard so often they feel like physics. Step outside it, or listen to someone who did not grow up inside it, and the clean idea of a universally pretty chord gets harder to defend. The music of the spheres is a beautiful metaphor. It is a bad description of what a chord does to a person. The week after this, on [What Babies Listen To](https://lukefwalton.com/love-music-more/episodes/what-babies-listen-to/), I ask the question from the other direction: what a two-day-old brain already does with a phrase. Consonance is the piece I still will not call innate.

faq
  • question
    Do good chords and bad chords exist?
    answer
    Not as a law of nature, on the argument I make here. Western theory ranks some intervals as consonant and some as dissonant, and a Western ear often agrees. Listening among the Tsimane' of the Bolivian Amazon does not simply confirm that ranking. Pleasantness looks trained.
  • question
    Who are the Tsimane' in this episode?
    answer
    An Indigenous people in the Bolivian Amazon. I use research that brought headphones to communities reachable by canoe, and I try not to turn them into a time capsule. Their songs carry stories. The point is what their listening does to the idea that a chord is naturally pleasant.
  • question
    What is the fishbowl?
    answer
    The culture you cannot see because you are in it. Harmony pedagogy, recordings, the intervals heard so often they feel like physics. The episode is about noticing that water. The following week, What Babies Listen To, asks what is already there before those years of training.
