hostNote
I can take a guitar part somewhere else without changing the tone. I only change how I hit it. A pick is loud. Fingers are quiet, and there is a lot between those two. Palm muting is the same decision: a big open chord, the AC/DC kind, or that chord choked so the strings stop ringing on their own. That is why I will never fully master the instrument. There are too many sounds. I was a better, more technical guitarist right after college. I am a more interesting one now, because I treat the guitar the way I treat a mix. I decide fast, and I try to stay out of my own head. The earlier case for the instrument is [The Beautiful Electric Guitar](https://lukefwalton.com/love-music-more/episodes/the-beautiful-electric-guitar/). One string is closer to a voice. Jumping strings shifts the register. Staying on one string lets the line flow up and down, the way a vocal cord does. I am not good at slide, so I emulate that slide on the guitar I can actually play. Same move with [the oud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oud). Working with [Tamtam](https://lukefwalton.com/with/tamtam/), I was around people who played it, and I wanted the microtonality: the places between the notes, and the way you get from one to the next, not only the blue notes. I cannot play the oud. I can drip the octatonic scale in and out as spice without reinventing how I see the notes. Inside the blues and rock solos I already know, I can channel a little Radiohead: color notes between the notes that feel like home. The pentatonic is home. Five notes, hard to wreck, and easy to move, including into the "wrong" key if I am clever, if I follow my ear, or if I get a happy mistake. [Part-writing](https://lukefwalton.com/love-music-more/episodes/part-writing-turning-notes-and-chords-into-music/) is the longer version of turning those notes into a line. CAGED is the cheat. I move the same chord shape up the neck and the shape comes back, so changing key is a matter of where the shape sits. Knowing the shape and knowing the notes is better than either one. I can play the same thing up in whole steps, barely move my hands, and get a rise that still sits on a lot of music. The tuning is the quirk. Unlike a violin, the intervals stay even and then they jump, so some scales are easier on a violin and some shapes are easier here. Two notes, a dyad, with downstrokes can point backward. One little triangle of a shape takes me to classic rock, to "Here Comes the Sun." I have learned a thousand songs that use it. The D shape runs through songs from The Who to the Beatles, whether or not the listener knows that. The pentatonic is the expectation, which is why it is the place I leave from. A lot of the jazz I like does bluesy things in an unexpected way. Pickups decide which part of the string I am sampling. The back pickup and the front pickup make me hit the guitar differently. When the tone already sounds like everyone else, I have more room to put my personality in the notes. I play to the microphone, not to the amp, and not to the sound hole of an acoustic. What the microphone hears is what gets captured. Closer or farther changes the fingers, the squeak, and the body, and the same idea works on a voice. Then the part itself: how much of the spectrum, one note or several. Often the right answer is fewer notes. I can layer, or I can spread a busy idea the way an orchestra does, keys on these notes and guitar on those. A chord chart makes me play every note. A part should sing and leave room, because most notes do not need to be repeated. Sometimes the coolest choice is to leave out the root. Fingers or a pick, hard or soft, muted or open. Most instruments have a version of those choices. The guitar just has a lot of them.

faq
  • question
    How do I make a guitar part more interesting without changing the tone?
    answer
    I change how I hit it. A pick is louder than fingers, and there is a lot between those two. Palm muting stops the strings from ringing. I was a more technical guitarist right after college. I get more interesting parts now by deciding fast, the way I mix, and staying out of my own head.
  • question
    What is the CAGED system?
    answer
    It is a way to move one chord shape up and down the neck and have the shape come back. Changing key is easier when I think in shapes. It is better if I also know the note names. The guitar's tuning makes this quirkier than a violin, because the intervals stay even and then they jump.
  • question
    Should a guitar play every note in the chord?
    answer
    A chord chart will make me play all of them. A part should sing and leave room. Often the right answer is fewer notes, so the keys can take some and the guitar can take others. Most notes do not need to be repeated. Sometimes the coolest choice is to leave out the root.
  • question
    Why play to the microphone instead of the amp?
    answer
    What the microphone hears is what gets captured. I can get a better performance by playing to that, not to the amp in the room and not to the sound hole of an acoustic. The back pickup and the front pickup make me hit the guitar differently. Each one is a choice about which part of the string gets sampled.
