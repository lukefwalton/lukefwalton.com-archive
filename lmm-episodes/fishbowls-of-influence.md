hostNote: |
  Do good chords exist? Bad ones? If they did, how would anyone know? I take the case of the Tsimane', an Indigenous people in the Bolivian Amazon, and what their listening does to a Western idea of which chords are naturally pleasant.

  The comparison is the one music theory treats as settled: consonant against dissonant, the intervals a Western ear is taught to hear as pretty or tense. Headphones go in. The result I care about is a different psychoacoustic response, not a confirmation of the textbook ranking. Preference can be a growing habit inside a tradition. A bunch of neat ratios does not settle it. The Tsimane' are not a time capsule, and not a control group to talk over. Their songs carry stories. Communities in this work are reachable by canoe. The lesson is that "pleasant" is something a culture trains, not something a chord possesses.

  I call the surrounding condition a fishbowl. You do not notice the water. Western ears swim in a harmony pedagogy, a recording tradition, a set of intervals heard so often they feel like physics. Step outside it, or listen to someone who did not grow up inside it, and the clean idea of a universally pretty chord gets harder to defend. The music of the spheres is a beautiful metaphor. It is a bad description of what a chord does to a person.

  The week after this, on [What Babies Listen To](https://lukefwalton.com/love-music-more/episodes/what-babies-listen-to/), I ask the question from the other direction: what a two-day-old brain already does with a phrase. Consonance is the piece I still will not call innate.
