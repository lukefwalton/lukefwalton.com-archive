hostNote
Carey Clayton’s first album under the name Modern Olympia is called **Justification Machine**. The songs keep coming back to the mental hoops people jump through to tell themselves something is okay. On lyrics, he trusts the lines he doesn’t fully understand yet. The clever ones feel contrived. Touring, session work, and TV and film production music are how he learns what makes a genre tick, including music he would not have chosen, and carries that back into his own songs. A solo session turns into an echo chamber, so he still wants other people’s takes. The quick test for “done,” nothing left that’s wrong, is the one he doesn’t like. He’d rather know the song is good, and when an idea shows up he tries to write, record, and produce it in an afternoon. We spend the middle of the hour on the live work. Morgan Jay’s show stays fresh after about thirty dates in under a year because so much of it is crowd work: they vamp and leave space for the audience’s punch line. Great Good Fine Ok is the longer story. He knew a member from Miami, met drummer Danny and singer John again in New York, and [Russ Flynn](https://lukefwalton.com/love-music-more/episodes/russ-flynn/) called him in about ten years ago, after Flynn had been subbing with X Ambassadors. The China songs are a different path. An Instagram message led to producing an artist who was in Los Angeles, then to something like eighty songs over a few years, written in English with John and pitched by an A&R contact after translation. He does not go to China to write them. Great Good Fine Ok did tour China, and he says that trip had nothing to do with these songs. Two of the songs became number one for a while, which he treats as a goofy fun fact. The gigs, he says, came from people he likes making music with, not from auditions. Asked why he loves music, he talks about rooms he would not otherwise have entered: a record in Nigeria, a choreography project in Istanbul, and the quieter work of sitting with a sound until it says something about him.
