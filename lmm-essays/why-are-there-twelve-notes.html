First, let's time-travel back to Ancient Greece.

The West Loves Ratios

In the West, humanity's search for meaning and order is wrapped up alongside the notion of beauty.

Some of the greatest mathematical minds of all ages — Pythagoras and Euclid in ancient Greece, through Leonardo of Pisa and Kepler, to present-day scientific figures — have spent endless hours over this simple ratio and its properties. It is probably fair to say that the Golden Ratio has inspired thinkers of all disciplines like no other number in the history of mathematics.

But the point isn't whether or not the Golden Ratio is, in fact, divine. This isn't a math post. Instead, I'm trying to share the frame of mind from which Western music was derived.

In other words: a desire for order. For reason. For divinity in the notes and the world, regardless if it's really there.

Pythagoras and Ancient Greek Tuning

"Mr. A-squared" was a mystic-meets-mathematician, searching for the math behind beauty. He and his followers developed Greek tuning.

Naturally, they developed their system around the overtone series.

But what are overtones? In Tuvan throat singing, the vocalist sings a fixed low pitch and changes the shape of their mouth and throat to control overtones that appear above the fundamental. It's like they're singing two notes at once — one with their vocal cords, the other with their mouth shape.

On a string, the overtone series is the set of frequencies that ring when you divide the string in halves, thirds, fourths, and so on.

<figure> <img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F04756165-49a8-4385-b961-d6d1d6ecdf56_1920x1080.jpeg" alt="The harmonic series on a string" loading="eager" /> </figure>

Consider the oldest pitch-based ancient instruments. Archaeologists have found simple flutes from about 50,000 years ago. Many of these early flutes used the light, hollow bones of a bird. Holes would be drilled so the player could alter the length of the air column inside the flute with their fingers.

<figure> <img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa9a654e1-afa4-4c43-9851-d97a7da9416e_220x330.jpeg" alt="An early bone flute" loading="eager" /> <figcaption>Toot toot.</figcaption> </figure>

On an early lyre (kinda like a harp), adjusting the tension on a string changes how it vibrates.

<figure> <img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8e07253c-aad1-4796-b27f-501d4423cffb_699x1000.jpeg" alt="The Sumerian Queen's lyre, an early lyre" loading="eager" /> </figure>

The Greeks likely did not have polyphony, or harmony in the way we conceptualize it. Plato's writings say that most Greek music consisted of single melodies without harmonies. Research suggests much of it was poetry accompanied by the lyre or the aulos (I want one).

There are some fragments in Euripides' play Orestes indicating simultaneous notes and complex rhythms. But we're not sure how to play them, since their concept of musical notation wasn't quite like ours.

The "Perfect" Octave (1:2 Ratio)

The octave is a truly incredible thing. I even got a bit spiritual about it in the last post.

It's the doubling of a frequency. Any frequency. It's what allows us to translate things like an earthquake into the range of human hearing, or a bee's wings. We octave-shift up or down.

It's also the first and strongest overtone in the harmonic series (like the Tuvan throat singers were using). So nature itself is kinda screaming about how awesome octaves are.

And here's the key point: we perceive octaves to be the same pitch despite their difference in frequency.

That's what allows a C to be a C regardless of how high or low it is. Middle C vibrates at 261.626 Hz in our standard, modern system. The octave above is (261.626 × 2) ≈ 523.251 Hz.

<figure> <img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F365164be-3b59-4174-8208-966786c287e6_706x153.png" alt="Octave frequency doubling from middle C" loading="eager" /> </figure>

The repeating patterns of a keyboard each reflect octaves: changes in frequency up and down, but not in pitch.

<figure> <img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2d19f27b-6786-4669-915f-40276bef8683_667x337.png" alt="Repeating octave patterns on a keyboard" loading="eager" /> </figure>

The first two notes ("some" and "where") of "Somewhere Over the Rainbow" are octaves. Same note. The higher note vibrates twice as fast as the first.

So We've Got Octaves (2:1). Let's Add Fifths (3:2)

Expanding on this idea of perfect ratios, we add the ratio of 3:2. Between the octaves and this new ratio (the Perfect Fifth!), we can derive all 12 musical notes.

Feel free to let your eyes glaze over.

Let's start with C. A perfect fifth away from C, we call a G.

Let's go all the way around. Notice how after 12 notes, it ends up back where we started:

C to G; G to D; D to A; A to E; E to B; B to F♯; F♯ to C♯; C♯ to G♯ (aka A♭, same thing in our system); A♭ to E♭; E♭ to B♭; B♭ to F; F returns to C.

This is called the circle of fifths.

In the next post I dig deeper into that circle. It's a daunting musical diagram, but a very powerful way to understand Western music on its own terms.
